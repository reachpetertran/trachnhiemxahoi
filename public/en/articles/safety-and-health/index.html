Occupational safety and health: health checks, occupational diseases and what you can see yourself

Checked on 18/11/2026 · Compiled by Peter Tran

In short
  • Employers must provide a health check at least once a year; every 6 months for heavy or hazardous work – paid by the employer.
  • People exposed to dust, noise or chemicals must be screened for occupational diseases; women get a gynaecological check.
  • 35 occupational diseases are covered by social insurance – for example pneumoconiosis, noise-induced hearing loss, lead and benzene poisoning.

Quotations of Vietnamese law below are unofficial translations. The Vietnamese text prevails.

1. Health checks: how often, and who pays

Law on Occupational Safety and Health 2015, Article 21: 1. Every year, the employer shall organise a health check at least once for employees; employees doing heavy, hazardous or dangerous work, or especially heavy, hazardous or dangerous work, employees with disabilities, minors and older employees shall have a health check at least once every 06 months. 2. During these health checks, female employees shall receive a gynaecological examination, and people working in environments exposed to factors that may cause occupational diseases shall be screened for occupational diseases. 3. The employer shall organise a health check before assigning work, before moving an employee to heavier, more hazardous or more dangerous work, and after recovery from a work accident or occupational disease before returning to work…

The employer pays for the checks. You have the right to ask for your own results.

Common problems in factories: only a general check on hiring; no occupational disease screening for people in dusty, noisy or chemical areas; no gynaecological check for women; people in heavy work checked only once a year.

2. 35 occupational diseases covered by social insurance

Since 15/02/2026, the list is set by Circular 60/2025/TT-BYT (replacing Circulars 15/2016 and 02/2023) and still has 35 diseases (Article 3). Some common in factories:

  • silicosis, asbestosis, byssinosis (cotton dust), talc and coal pneumoconiosis; occupational asthma and chronic bronchitis;
  • noise-induced hearing loss; diseases from whole-body and hand-arm vibration;
  • poisoning by lead, benzene and its homologues, mercury, manganese, cadmium, carbon monoxide, arsenic;
  • occupational skin diseases (from chromium, natural rubber and rubber additives, long exposure to wet or cold conditions);
  • decompression sickness (divers).

If you have worked for years with dust, noise or chemicals and have symptoms (long-lasting cough, shortness of breath, hearing loss…), tell the doctor about your job and workplace, and ask about occupational disease screening.

3. The right to refuse dangerous work

Law on Occupational Safety and Health 2015, Article 6(1)(đ): employees have the right to “refuse to do the work or leave the workplace, while still being paid in full and without being considered in breach of labour discipline, when they clearly see a risk of a work accident seriously threatening their life or health, but must immediately inform their direct manager so that a solution can be arranged…”

Article 6 also gives you the right to be informed of hazards at work and how to prevent them, to be trained in safety, to be screened for occupational diseases, and to be covered by work accident and occupational disease insurance. In return you must use the protective equipment provided and follow safety procedures.

4. Things you can see for yourself

These are points auditors usually check in factories. You can check them too:

  • Emergency exits are not locked or blocked by goods; there are exit signs and emergency lights.
  • An evacuation map is posted in work areas; there is an assembly point sign with enough space for everyone.
  • Fire drills include the night shift, not only the day shift.
  • Fire extinguishers are on brackets or in cabinets – not on the floor, not hidden.
  • Safety data sheets (SDS/MSDS) are kept where chemicals are stored. The 2025 Law on Chemicals (Article 25) requires buyers of hazardous chemicals to request and keep these sheets at the storage location. A Vietnamese version should be available so you can read it.
  • Forklifts have an audible warning when entering danger zones (QCVN 25:2015/BLĐTBXH).
  • Machines and equipment with strict safety requirements (boilers, pressure vessels, lifts, cranes, forklifts…) carry valid inspection stickers.
  • A complete first-aid kit and a trained first-aider on every shift.

5. After a work accident: what to do straight away

  1. Get help first: call 115 if serious. Tell your manager immediately.
  2. Write it down: time, place, the machine or task, witnesses. Take photos of the scene if safe.
  3. Keep all medical papers: discharge papers, receipts, assessment results.
  4. Ask the company about reporting the work accident and about benefits from the employer and from the work accident and occupational disease insurance fund. Exact compensation amounts will be covered in a separate article after checking the original texts.
  5. If refused, see Keep evidence and complain the right way.

Vietnam has ratified ILO Convention No. 155 on occupational safety and health (1994) and No. 187 on the promotional framework for occupational safety and health (2014). Both are now ILO fundamental conventions.

Coming changes

A bill amending the Law on Occupational Safety and Health was submitted to the National Assembly on 21/10/2026, expected to take effect on 01/03/2027. Main points: renaming authorities under the new government structure, revising accident reporting and investigation, cutting procedures. Not law yet.
