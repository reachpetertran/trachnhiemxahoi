Mental health: early signs, where to get care, what family and co-workers can do

Checked on 24/11/2026 · Compiled by Peter Tran

In short
  • Feeling sad, empty or losing interest in everything for at least 2 weeks, most of the day, together with poor sleep, tiredness, poor concentration or feeling worthless, is a sign to see a health worker. Mental illness is treatable, and earlier is better.
  • You can start at the commune/ward health station, a hospital's psychiatry department, or the provincial psychiatric hospital. People with serious mental illness can apply for a disability certificate (“neurological and mental” type) and an allowance.
  • If someone says they want to die or plans to harm themselves: do not leave them alone, move dangerous items away, call 115 or take them to the nearest emergency department.

This article helps you recognise problems and find help. It does not replace seeing a doctor. Quotations of Vietnamese law are unofficial translations; the Vietnamese text prevails.

1. Signs to watch for

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), a depressive episode involves:

  • Two core signs: a sad, irritable or empty mood; loss of interest or pleasure in activities you used to enjoy.
  • Other signs: poor concentration; excessive guilt or feeling worthless; hopelessness about the future; thoughts about dying or suicide; disrupted sleep; changes in appetite or weight; feeling very tired or low in energy.
  • Lasting at least 2 weeks, most of the day, nearly every day.

Other signs that need a check-up: hearing or seeing things others do not; suspecting without reason that someone wants to harm you; speaking or acting very differently from usual; drinking or using drugs more and more; fear or panic so strong you cannot go to work.

Workers on long night shifts, heavy overtime, far from family, in debt, after losing a job or after a work accident are more exposed to long-term stress. This is not “weakness”. It is a health problem and deserves care like any other illness.

2. When to get emergency help now

  • The person says they want to die, says goodbye, gives away valued things, or looks for ways to harm themselves.
  • The person is severely agitated, breaking things, and may harm others.
  • Confusion, high fever, seizures, or after taking medicine or chemicals.

What to do: do not leave them alone; calmly move medicines, knives, ropes and chemicals out of reach; call 115 or take them to the nearest emergency department. If others are in danger: call 113.

Law on Medical Examination and Treatment 2023, Article 82(1)(b): cases of compulsory treatment include “a person with depression who has suicidal ideas or behaviour; a person with a mental illness in an agitated state who may harm themselves, or who harms others or destroys property”.

So in these cases treatment is compulsory, to protect the person and those around them. The Government sets out the detailed measures (Article 82(2)).

3. Where to get care

  • Commune/ward health station: the nearest place to start; ask about the local community mental-health programme.
  • The psychiatry or neurology department of the provincial or city general hospital.
  • The provincial or city psychiatric hospital.

Bring your health insurance card (or ID card/VNeID). Mental-health care uses health insurance like other illnesses, following the rules on your registered clinic and referrals (see Health insurance) – being checked: specific rules on which psychiatric medicines are covered.

Time off for treatment: you receive sickness benefit if you pay compulsory social insurance (see Social insurance). Ask the health facility for discharge papers or a sick-leave certificate for social insurance.

4. Rights of patients and workers

  • Serious, long-term mental illness can count as a neurological and mental disability (Law on Persons with Disabilities, Article 3(1)(d)). Apply to the commune People's Committee for a disability assessment (Article 18). If assessed as severe or very severe: a monthly allowance of VND 810,000–1,350,000 (Decree 335/2026/NĐ-CP, Article 6). See People with disabilities.
  • Stigmatising or discriminating against people with disabilities is prohibited (Law on Persons with Disabilities, Article 14(1)). The Labour Code also bans discrimination on grounds of disability (Article 3(8); see Discrimination and harassment).
  • An employer may end a contract for sickness only after 12 months' continuous treatment (indefinite contract), 6 months (12–36-month contract) or more than half the contract term (contract under 12 months) without recovery; once the worker recovers, the employer considers re-hiring them (Labour Code, Article 36(1)(b)).

5. Common problems

  • Families hiding or locking up a sick relative for fear of what neighbours will say.
  • Relying only on spiritual healers, or stopping medicine as soon as things feel better – relapse is common.
  • Managers calling a worker “lazy” or “crazy” in front of others; sick leave for mental illness counted as unauthorised absence.
  • Nobody asking when a co-worker suddenly withdraws, comes in late, or cries at work.
  • Using alcohol to “forget” troubles.

6. What to do

If you are not feeling okay:

  1. Talk to someone you trust: family, a friend, your team leader, the company health worker.
  2. Get care as in section 3. Mental illness is treatable.
  3. WHO also recommends: keep doing things you used to enjoy, stay in touch with friends and family, exercise regularly, keep regular eating and sleeping times, and avoid or cut down on alcohol and drugs.

If you are a family member or co-worker:

  1. Ask directly and gently: “How have you been feeling lately?” Listen; do not blame; do not say “stop overthinking”.
  2. Ask whether they have thought about harming themselves. Research has not found that asking increases suicidal thoughts; being able to talk may help them feel lighter (Dazzi et al., 2014).
  3. Go with them to a health facility. Remind them to take medicine and attend follow-ups.
  4. Immediate danger: follow section 2.

7. Who to call

  • Thoughts of self-harm, life in danger: 115 or the nearest emergency department.
  • Danger to others: 113.
  • A child is abused or in crisis: 111.
  • Domestic violence: 117.
  • Check-ups and long-term care: commune/ward health station; provincial psychiatric hospital.
